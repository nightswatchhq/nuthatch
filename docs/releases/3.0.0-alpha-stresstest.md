# 3.0.0-alpha: the stress test before it becomes a release

RFC-0041 is the 2026 freeze's one carve-out, so authored incremental entities are the year's only new
capability and 3.0.0 rides entirely on them. This is the plan for proving that in the open before the
alpha becomes a release, written before the alpha is cut so the bar is set by what we want to know
rather than by what the runs happen to show.

**Nothing here is a formality.** Every item is a number that does not exist yet, and two of them could
send the work back.

## What is already proved, and where

So the list below is only the gaps.

| | evidence | where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the compiler, the circuit, the binder | unit + mutation | CI, both platforms |
| reorg convergence at any depth | property test through `spawn_nest` | CI |
| a dead entity quarantines its own nest | e2e, mutation-proved | CI |
| warm-restart seed correctness | e2e against sealed + hot | CI |
| per-cursor RSS with entities | 209 MB against a 2,048 MB budget | one run, 32-core Linux |
| per-row and per-circuit cost | 3,123 bytes; 3.5 MB | one run, 32-core Linux |

Everything in that table is a **captured corpus or a synthetic chain**. Nothing in it has met live
RPC, a real tip, or a process that has been up for days.

## The four things this has to answer

### 1. What a warm-restart seed costs on a real nest's history

The seed reads a table's whole sealed history into memory and folds it through the circuit. Every
test of it runs over eight to fourteen blocks. The Horizon capture is **10,135 segments and 381 MB**,
and that is what a real nest looks like.

Measure: seed time, peak RSS during the seed, and the live input row count against the declared
`max_rows`.

**This can fail the release.** `max_rows` applies to the seed like any other window, so an entity
whose history exceeds its declared bound **cannot restart**. That is deliberate - it could not have
been running in the first place - but if a plausible bound and a real history collide, the bound's
default guidance is wrong and the docs have to say so before anyone declares one.

Runs on the ThinkPad against the captured corpus. Needs no RPC and no VPS.

### 2. Whether an entity survives live tip-following

Everything so far is a tape source: blocks arrive when the test says so, reorgs happen on cue, and
the RPC never rate-limits. The pilot nests take about five `HTTP 429`s an hour from the free Arbitrum
endpoint and recover; an entity has never seen one.

Measure: an entity on a live nest at tip for days. Watch `applied_through` against the nest's head,
the `/ready` entity block, and whether a throttled window ever leaves the entity behind without
recovering.

Runs on the ThinkPad. A new nest on the staking contract, its own directory, `--backfill` from a
recent block rather than from the deployment.

**Status 2026-08-28, 20 hours in: holding, and checked rather than believed.** The nest is a USDC
`Transfer` nest on Arbitrum against the paid Alchemy endpoint, with one entity
`SELECT t.to, SUM(t.value) FROM usdc__transfer t GROUP BY t.to`. `applied_through` equals the nest's
head, and has at no sample been behind it.

The entity's own `current: true` is not evidence, so the relation was compared against a full
recompute over the raw table at the same block:

| | groups | total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| entity `received`, DBSP-maintained | 87,081 | 1,568,436,272,938,895 |
| `SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT "to"), SUM(value) FROM usdc__transfer` | 87,081 | 1,568,436,272,938,895 |

Both at `as_of` 499292505, so they are comparable rather than merely close. A parallel Ethereum nest
took a **real reorg** during the window and the relation converged: 32 rows retracted, after which it
summed to `8332934332287503`, identical to every decoded transfer.

Still to run: the remaining hours, and a throttled window - the paid endpoint has not rate-limited us
once, so the specific thing this item set out to watch has not actually happened yet. That is worth
saying plainly rather than counting the hours as if it had.

### 3. A restart of a process that has been up for days

Not the same test as #1. That one measures the seed against a big corpus; this one asks whether a
long-lived process with an entity comes back correctly - a real restart, real sealing having happened
underneath it, real hot tail.

The ThinkPad cannot answer this on its own: a laptop sleeps and gets rebooted, and "it survived a
week at tip" is not a claim you can make on one. This is the item the **Hetzner box** exists for, and
the reason the two-box rule is a rule.

### 4. Whether the two admission constants hold up

`ENTITY_RSS_BYTES_PER_ROW` (3,200) and `ENTITY_CIRCUIT_RSS_MB` (8) are **one measurement each, on one
machine**, and they gate mount admission. Getting them wrong in the safe direction refuses a mount the
hardware would have handled; getting them wrong in the unsafe direction admits one that then breaches
a budget CLAUDE.md calls a non-negotiable.

The per-row figure in particular is argued to be conservative because fixed allocations dominate at
889 rows. **That is reasoning, not a second measurement.**

Measure: the footprint pair again on a second machine, and once at a materially larger row count.

**Status 2026-08-28: the first attempt measured the wrong thing, and the correction matters more than
the number.** Comparing the entity nest's RSS against a no-entity control on the same chain looked
straightforward and was not: the two processes are different ages, so a constant offset from their
allocator histories swamped the difference - at one point the difference read *negative*, which is
physically impossible for a strictly-added relation.

Sampling both on the same tick and reading the *slope* was meant to cancel that offset. It does not,
because there is a sawtooth larger than the signal. The entity process was observed at 396 MB and then
at 266 MB four minutes later, a ~130 MB release; a slope taken across a trough gives **40,861 bytes per
new row** against a charged budget of 3,200, and the same data's flat second half gives 13,943. Both
figures are the allocator, not the entity. **Any bytes-per-row number from a window shorter than the
sawtooth is measuring the sawtooth.**

What the live run can honestly say, and it is the part the budget actually cares about: at **88,579
rows** the entity nest sits at **315 MB** against a no-entity control at **364 MB**, on a 2,048 MB
per-cursor budget. The entity's cost is smaller than the difference between two differently-aged copies
of the same program, and the cursor is at roughly 16% of budget.

So `ENTITY_RSS_BYTES_PER_ROW` is still **one measurement on one machine**. The controlled version -
both processes started at the same instant, run to the same block - has not been done, because doing it
means restarting the nest that is currently carrying item 2.

## The order, and why

1. **ThinkPad, captured corpus** - the seed measurement. Free, immediate, and the item most likely to
   send the work back. Do it first for exactly that reason.
2. **ThinkPad, live nest** - tip-following with an entity, days.
3. **Hetzner** - a short-lived service, days not weeks, for the long-running restart and the two-box
   rule. Only after the ThinkPad runs are boring.

**Adding an `entities.toml` to an existing pilot nest is not on this list.** It changes that nest's
restart behaviour from "come back up" to "seed from the whole sealed corpus first", and doing that to
the Oracle nest in production before item 1 has produced a number is the wrong order.

## What 3.0.0 is signalling

The upgrade is **drop-in**: replace the binary, restart. No config migration, no data migration, no
re-index, and a nest that declares no entities behaves exactly as it did on 2.7.x.

The major bump says *this is significant*, not *this will break you*. A 2.x to 3.0 jump sends
operators looking for a migration guide, so the release notes' first job is to say there is nothing to
migrate.

The one genuinely new behaviour for an existing nest: **adding an `entities.toml` to a nest that
already has history means its next restart seeds from the whole sealed corpus.** Item 1 is what tells
us whether that sentence needs a warning next to it.

## The bar

The alpha becomes a release when items 1 to 4 have produced numbers, those numbers are in
`docs/bench/`, and the entity has run at tip on both boxes without falling behind and staying behind.

Not "the tests pass" - they already do. Numbers, on hardware, from runs someone can point at.
